# Methodology

The ten questions that decide whether an unsupervised time-series result means anything,
answered for this project.

---

### 1. What is the observational unit?

Three nested ones, and confusing them is the most common way this kind of project goes wrong.

- A **row** is one machine-minute with 38 values.
- A **window** of 2500 rows is what a detector judges. Drift is a property of a distribution,
  so a single row cannot be "drifted".
- A **stream** — one machine × scenario × magnitude × seed — is what metrics and bootstrap
  resampling aggregate over.

Windows within a stream overlap by 2000 of 2500 rows. Treating them as independent evidence
would shrink every confidence interval to a number with no meaning.

### 2. How is the target defined?

There is no target. The system is unsupervised: it compares a window against a reference and
emits a scalar. Ground truth exists only for evaluation, and it is generated by us —
`(onset, drift type, affected features, magnitude)` — never shown to any detector.

### 3. What exactly is available to the model at inference time?

The reference sample and the current window. Nothing else. In particular:

- no ground-truth onset, type or magnitude;
- no future rows beyond the window's last index;
- no cross-machine information — every detector is fitted per stream;
- no anomaly labels.

The one global quantity a detector uses is its threshold, which comes from that machine's own
earlier clean history (§ 6).

### 4. Are there features that only exist after the target event?

No, and the structure makes it hard to introduce one. Features are the raw telemetry columns;
there is no engineered feature computed over a horizon that extends past the window. The
per-feature standard deviation used to scale injected magnitudes is measured on the
**pre-onset** slice, so the injection itself cannot borrow information from the drifted region.

### 5. Can one entity appear in both train and test?

A machine appears in exactly one of calibration / development / held-out.

A machine *does* appear in both its own threshold calibration and its own evaluation, and this
is deliberate: baselining per entity is how the method is meant to be deployed. It is made
legitimate by time — calibration reads the first 35% of a machine's clean history, evaluation
the remaining 65%, with no overlap. Enforced in `data.smd.segment_bounds`, tested in
`tests/test_data.py`.

### 6. Can information from the future reach the past?

Three places where it could, and what stops it:

- **Reference windows.** `ReferenceTracker` only ever returns rows strictly before the scored
  window. The `sliding` and `reset_on_alarm` policies move the reference forward, never back.
- **Threshold calibration.** Earlier-in-time half of the same machine, never the evaluation half.
- **Dataset normalisation.** SMD ships values min-max scaled over the whole series by its
  authors. This is a real, inherited leak. It is a monotone per-column rescaling and it affects
  all methods identically, so it cannot create a spurious difference between detectors — but it
  means absolute scale here is not a quantity anyone should trust, and no statistic in the
  project depends on it.

### 7. Is preprocessing fitted only on training data?

Yes. Every fitted object — PCA basis, standardisation, PSI bin edges, histogram edges,
classifier — is fitted inside `fit_reference` on the reference sample alone and then applied
to the window. PSI bin edges in particular are frozen with the reference; recomputing them per
window would compare each sample against its own bins and drive the statistic toward zero
exactly when the distribution moves.

### 8. Does offline evaluation match the deployment scenario?

Closely, with two honest gaps.

Matching: streaming order is preserved; the reference always precedes the window; thresholds
come from historical clean data for that machine; false alarms are counted at a fixed operating
budget rather than at an oracle-chosen threshold.

Gaps:

- **One change per stream.** Real systems drift repeatedly and drift back. Only the
  `reset_on_alarm` policy study touches the multi-change case.
- **Injected changes are cleanly parameterised.** A real regression is messier than a shift of
  *k* sigma on six columns. This is the price of having ground truth at all, and it is why the
  Insects streams — with drift induced physically rather than arithmetically — matter as
  external validation.

### 9. What assumptions do the conclusions rest on?

1. SMD's `train` half is drift-free. Inherited from the dataset, not verified. If it is not,
   the measured false-alarm rate is pessimistic, not optimistic.
2. Injected drift is representative enough of real drift for relative detector rankings to
   transfer. Partly testable via the external validation, not fully.
3. The autocorrelation estimator's 0.3 threshold is a reasonable operating point. It is a
   measured compromise, not a convention: at 0.1 the required thinning step is 231–1124 rows
   and almost no data survives; at 0.3 it is 73–191 and ~15–35 effective samples remain in a
   2500-row window. Both numbers come from the fleet and either is defensible; the choice is
   stated rather than hidden.
4. Streams are independent enough for a bootstrap over them to be valid. Streams from the same
   machine share a substrate, so the intervals are mildly optimistic. Resampling machines
   instead would be stricter and would leave 11 units.

### 10. Where will this break?

- **Slow drift under a moving reference.** A sliding or reset-on-alarm reference absorbs a slow
  ramp: each acceptance moves the baseline, and the cumulative change is never compared against
  anything. Measured directly in the policy study.
- **Small subgroups.** A change confined to a few percent of entities is unlikely to be visible
  at fleet level at any tolerable false-alarm rate.
- **Non-stationarity mistaken for drift.** SMD's clean half is itself far from stationary. A
  detector tuned aggressively will spend its whole budget on natural variation. This is why
  every threshold is empirical and per machine.
- **Latency floor.** With τ ≈ 150 minutes, nothing here reacts in under roughly 40 hours of
  telemetry. A faster requirement needs a different class of method — sequential change-point
  detection on a low-dimensional summary — not a better threshold.

## Pre-registered predictions and how they turned out

Recorded before the experiments were run, so the outcomes could not be rationalised afterwards.
Four of six held. The two that failed are reported in the README with the reasoning that was
wrong rather than quietly removed.

| # | Prediction | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Marginal statistics score **exactly** the same before and after `correlation_break`, because the permutation preserves every marginal bit-for-bit | **Partly wrong.** True of the aggregate post-onset region — asserted by test — but a scored window is a subsample of it, and permutation moves values between windows. `wasserstein_max` reaches 0.620 [0.531, 0.700]. See REVIEW R2-7 |
| 2 | `pca_recon` detects `correlation_break`; the marginal family does not | **Held.** 0.891 [0.833, 0.946] against 0.48–0.62 |
| 3 | Untuned statistics saturate here, and thinning restores dynamic range | **Partly held.** Saturation is real (`ks_bonferroni` at exactly 0.500; five detectors have no usable operating point). Thinning helps only the binned divergences and costs the others up to 0.067 AUC |
| 4 | A sliding reference loses most of its power on `incremental_shift` | **Wrong.** It is the one scenario where sliding wins, 0.878 vs 0.849 |
| 5 | `iforest_mean`, a point scorer averaged over a window, is a mediocre drift detector | **Wrong.** It is the strongest detector in the study |
| 6 | Delay and false-alarm rate trade off, so a single-number leaderboard is misleading | **Held, and then some.** The AUC leader (`pca_recon`) is near the chance floor once a threshold must be chosen |
